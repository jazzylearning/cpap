Understanding Sleep Apnoea

A plain-language guide to Obstructive (OSA) and Central (CSA) Sleep Apnoea — how they differ, what they do to your body, and what actually helps — based on 17 peer-reviewed sources and a 380-page clinical monograph.

Medical disclaimer: This page is an educational summary of published research. It is not medical advice, cannot diagnose you, and does not replace a consultation, a sleep study, or your doctor's judgement. If you snore, feel exhausted, or someone tells you your breathing stops at night, please talk to a physician or sleep specialist.

  • 1 OSA is a mechanical problem — your throat physically collapses during sleep. CSA is a control/signalling problem — the brain's breathing "thermostat" temporarily stops sending the signal to breathe. They can look similar to a bed-partner but need different treatment. Schwarz 2024, p.310
  • 2 OSA is far more common than CSA; CSA is mostly seen alongside heart failure (up to 40–75% of heart-failure patients), high altitude, opioid use, or shortly after starting CPAP. Monograph, p.37
  • 3 CPAP is still the best-studied treatment for OSA and lowers blood pressure by roughly 2–3.5 mmHg, with the biggest drop happening at night — but only if it's actually worn (≥4–5 h/night). Benning 2025, p.3
  • 4 People who benefit most from CPAP's blood-pressure effect are those with already-high/uncontrolled blood pressure, severe OSA, and younger age — not everyone with OSA gets a big BP benefit. Pengo 2025, p.5
  • 5 For heart failure specifically, one PAP mode (adaptive servo-ventilation, ASV) can be harmful in the most severely weakened hearts (LVEF ≤45%) and is contraindicated there — a good example of "one size does not fit all". Schwarz 2024, p.315
  • 6 Telemedicine ("e-Sleep") can diagnose, titrate, and follow up sleep apnoea remotely about as well as in-person care, and modestly improves CPAP adherence (roughly +30–40 minutes/night in pooled analyses) — but it can't replace a physical exam and long-term data are still limited. Verbraecken 2025, p.5

2. OSA vs CSA — side by side

3. Why it happens: the airway and the breathing-control loop

OSA: a collapsible airway

Fat deposits around the throat, a naturally narrow airway, and loss of muscle tone during sleep let the soft palate and tongue fall backward, narrowing or completely blocking the airway. Monograph, p.44

Four overlapping factors ("endotypes") determine who gets OSA and how severe it is: (1) how collapsible the airway is anatomically (present to some degree in ~100% of patients), (2) how well the tongue/throat muscles compensate (impaired in ~36%), (3) how easily a person wakes up — the arousal threshold (low in ~37%, which stops muscles re-engaging), and (4) how unstable the breathing-control feedback loop is — loop gain (high in ~36%). Monograph, p.63

Every 5 kg/m² drop in BMI reduces the pressure needed to keep the airway open by about 5 cmH₂O. Monograph, p.67

CSA: an unstable breathing-control loop

Loop gain = how strongly the body over-reacts to a small change in breathing (a bit like an over-sensitive thermostat). When loop gain is greater than 1, the breathing system overshoots, the same way a thermostat that overcorrects can swing a room between too hot and too cold. Schwarz 2024, p.313

After a burst of over-breathing (hyperventilation), CO₂ falls below the "apnoeic threshold" — only about 3–5 mmHg below normal — and the drive to breathe switches off completely until CO₂ rebuilds. Monograph, p.72–73

In heart failure, a weaker heart pumps blood more slowly, so it takes longer for the brain's sensors to "see" a CO₂ change — this delay itself destabilises the loop, and fluid shifted overnight from the legs into the neck/lungs adds to the instability. Schwarz 2024, p.313–315

4. Effects on organs and systems

Blood pressure — the best-studied effect

Three large meta-analyses in this collection quantify how much CPAP lowers blood pressure, and — importantly — who benefits most. The reductions are real but modest on average; they matter most for people with high or hard-to-control blood pressure.

Data: Benning et al. 2025 (75 RCTs, 10,025 patients) — overall CPAP effect by blood-pressure measurement type. Benning 2025, p.3–4

Who benefits most

  • Higher/uncontrolled baseline blood pressure: CPAP–control SBP difference −2.57 mmHg vs ~0 mmHg in already-controlled/normotensive patients. Pengo 2025, p.5
  • Severe OSA (AHI≥30) and high hypoxic burden (SpO₂min ≤77%): SBP falls further (e.g. −3.32 mmHg vs weaker effect in less severe OSA). Pengo 2020, p.7
  • Younger age (≤60 years) shows a somewhat larger office/24h BP drop than older age groups. Pengo 2025, p.6
  • Using CPAP ≥5 h/night: 24h SBP −4.2 mmHg vs −2.1 mmHg for <5h/night. Benning 2025, p.6

A surprising exception

In patients who also have heart failure, CPAP's blood-pressure effect can reverse: across 3 trials, office SBP actually increased by +5.82 mmHg on CPAP, versus a −2.39 mmHg reduction in studies without heart failure (p=0.009) — a reminder that CPAP's cardiovascular effects are not uniform across all patients. Pengo 2020, p.6, 10

❤️ Heart & arrhythmia

CSA occurs in up to 40% of unselected heart-failure patients (75% in the most severe reduced-function cases). In the CANPAP trial, CPAP halved the apnoea index (40→19–21/h) and improved heart pumping (ejection fraction) by nearly 5%, but did not improve overall survival in the main analysis. Monograph, p.370, Schwarz 2024, p.314

Recurrent pressure swings and low oxygen promote structural changes in the heart's upper chambers, raising atrial fibrillation risk; a 10% rise in night-to-night breathing-disturbance variability was linked to an 89% higher risk of persistent AF. Monograph, p.259

🧠 Brain & stroke

OSA is an independent risk factor for stroke. Monograph, p.159

In a Korean cohort of 1,441 adults followed 8 years, moderate-to-severe OSA more than doubled the risk of new small brain bleeds ("cerebral microbleeds"): RR = 2.14 (95% CI 1.08–4.23). Siddiquee 2025, p.7–8

A large US veterans cohort (11M+ people) found OSA associated with a higher risk of incident Parkinson disease (HR 1.92); starting CPAP early cut that risk (HR 0.69) — about 439 people would need early CPAP to prevent one case of Parkinson's over 5 years. Neilson 2025, p.5

⚖️ Metabolism & weight

Weight and OSA reinforce each other, but not in a simple straight line: the first 10% of weight loss can cut AHI by more than 20–30%, with diminishing extra benefit beyond that. Monograph, p.33, 48

The newer weight-loss drug tirzepatide (SURMOUNT-OSA trials) produced 17.7–19.6% body-weight loss and cut AHI by 25.3–29.3 events/hour, leading to FDA approval specifically for obesity-related OSA — the first drug approved directly for OSA. Monograph, p.279

Bariatric surgery (>25–30% total weight loss) cuts AHI by ~19 events/hour on average, with OSA remission in roughly 26–76% (average ~65%) of patients — meaning many still have residual OSA afterward. Monograph, p.281–282

🧬 Hormones (testosterone)

Testosterone helps maintain muscle tone in the throat; low testosterone can worsen airway collapsibility, and low testosterone itself is linked to fatigue, low mood, and reduced concentration. Andersen 2026, p.7, 9

Thin/negative finding worth stating plainly: despite this connection, meta-analyses show CPAP does not significantly change testosterone levels — treating OSA does not reliably "fix" low testosterone. Andersen 2026, p.8

Testosterone replacement therapy, conversely, can worsen or trigger OSA and should prompt a sleep evaluation first in higher-risk patients. Andersen 2026, p.7–9

😵 Mood, cognition & sleepiness

In a Canadian cohort of over 30,000 adults aged 45–85, people at high risk of OSA had about 40% higher odds of a mental-health condition (both at one point in time and later), rising to ~44% higher odds when tracked repeatedly over time. Kendzerska 2025, p.7–8

CPAP improves subjective daytime sleepiness (Epworth Sleepiness Scale) by about 2.1 points on average in randomised trials. Monograph, p.117

🚗 Accidents

People with OSA have roughly 1.5–2.5 times the risk of motor-vehicle accidents compared with the general population; how sleepy someone feels predicts crash risk better than the AHI number alone. Monograph, p.113, 159

Europe-wide, driver sleepiness is estimated to be involved in around 20% of severe road-traffic accidents. Monograph, p.23

6. Telemedicine / "e-Sleep"

A 2025 European Respiratory Society statement (Verbraecken et al.) reviewed the evidence for remote/digital sleep-apnoea care across the whole care pathway. Importantly, this ERS statement is an evidence summary, not a clinical practice guideline — it does not issue formal recommendations. Verbraecken 2025, p.1

🩺 Diagnosis

Remote/attended home sleep testing with teletransmission of raw data reduces time to diagnosis and lowers travel/hospitalisation costs. Verbraecken 2025, p.4

📈 PAP titration

Unsupervised home auto-CPAP titration guided remotely achieves pressure settings and adherence comparable to in-lab titration. Verbraecken 2025, p.4, 12

🔁 Follow-up & adherence

Cloud-connected devices transmit nightly usage, leak, and residual AHI; remote troubleshooting improved adherence for 16/30 patients in one study, and pooled meta-analyses show +29 to +41 minutes/night of extra use versus usual care. 2025_ERS_CPAP compendium, p.3–5

✅ Benefits

  • Cost savings for patients (travel, lodging, missed work) and sometimes for the system: one RCT found ~37 CHF/patient savings. Verbraecken 2025, p.9
  • National scalability: in France, 97% of the country's 1.6 million CPAP users are managed via telemonitoring under a pay-for-performance scheme. Monograph, p.24

⚠️ Limitations

  • Cannot substitute for an in-person physical exam (e.g. cannot listen to the lungs remotely). Verbraecken 2025, p.11
  • Staff-time savings are inconsistent — some programmes actually increase clinician/nursing time from frequent remote contacts. Verbraecken 2025, p.1, 5, 9
  • A "ceiling effect" — telemedicine shows less advantage when the standard in-person clinic is already excellent. Verbraecken 2025, p.5, 12
  • Long-term (>12 month) adherence/termination data remain sparse; validated evidence is strongest for short-term (3–6 month) adult adherence. Verbraecken 2025, p.5, 10
  • Digital-access disparities: elderly, less-educated, or lower socioeconomic-status patients may be disadvantaged. Verbraecken 2025, p.6, 9–10
  • Complex patients with CSA (heart failure, stroke, kidney failure, atrial fibrillation) are validated by far fewer telemedicine studies, and remote tools must reliably tell CSA apart from OSA — which is not yet fully solved. Verbraecken 2025, p.11

7. Where more research/innovation is needed

📊 Individual-patient-data meta-analyses instead of aggregate/study-level data, to pin down exactly who benefits from CPAP and by how much. Pengo 2020, p.10–11

🧪 Prospective phenotype-driven trials confirming who truly benefits (age, baseline BP, hypoxic burden) rather than relying on subgroup analyses of existing trials. Benning 2025, p.9–10

💔 CSA is far less studied than OSA — there are simply fewer randomised trials, so many CSA treatment decisions rest on thinner evidence. Schwarz 2024, p.317

🫀 Re-evaluating ASV in heart failure — the newer, safer-looking ASV algorithms (ADVENT-HF) may prompt guideline revisions away from the older SERVE-HF-based caution. Schwarz 2024, p.315, 317

♀️ Sex-specific tools and representation — women made up only ~21.6% of participants in Benning's 75 pooled BP trials, and standard screening tools (STOP-Bang, Epworth) are less sensitive in women. Benning 2025, p.9–10

💊 Long-term safety of new weight-loss/OSA drugs — durability, muscle-mass loss, and true cardiovascular-outcome benefit of tirzepatide/GLP-1 drugs specifically in OSA populations remain to be proven over years, not months. Monograph, p.336–338

📡 Telemedicine for complex patients — validating remote pathways for heart failure, stroke, kidney failure, and atrial-fibrillation patients where CSA is common, plus resolving data-security/legal/reimbursement frameworks across countries. Verbraecken 2025, p.11, 13

📐 Standardising newer severity metrics (hypoxic burden, loop gain, heart-rate response) so they can be measured consistently and linked to real outcomes, moving beyond the simple AHI count. Monograph, p.129, 138

8. Glossary

AHI — Apnoea-Hypopnoea Index

Average number of breathing pauses (apnoeas) and shallow-breathing events (hypopnoeas) per hour of sleep. Used to grade severity: none <5, mild 5–15, moderate 15–30, severe ≥30 events/hour. Monograph, p.126

ODI — Oxygen Desaturation Index

Number of times per hour that blood oxygen saturation drops by a defined amount (commonly ≥3% or ≥4%). A companion measure to AHI reflecting how much oxygen actually falls. Monograph, p.127

PSG — Polysomnography

The full, "gold-standard" overnight sleep test, usually done in a lab, recording brain waves, eye movement, muscle activity, breathing, and oxygen levels. Can reliably tell obstructive from central events. Monograph, p.239

Polygraphy / HSAT (home sleep apnoea test)

A simplified, portable version of a sleep study, usually done at home, that records fewer signals (no brain waves) and cannot detect arousals — this tends to make AHI look lower than it truly is. Monograph, p.155, 239

CPAP — Continuous Positive Airway Pressure

A bedside machine that gently blows constant air pressure through a mask to keep the airway physically open all night. The first-line treatment for moderate-severe OSA. Monograph, p.70

ASV — Adaptive Servo-Ventilation

A "smart" PAP machine that continuously adjusts pressure support breath-by-breath to smooth out the surge-and-pause cycles of central sleep apnoea/Cheyne-Stokes respiration. Not safe for severely weakened hearts (LVEF ≤45%). Schwarz 2024, p.314–315

MAD — Mandibular Advancement Device

A custom-fitted oral appliance, similar to a mouthguard, that holds the lower jaw slightly forward during sleep to keep the airway open. Randerath 2021, p.6

Loop gain

A mathematical measure of how strongly the breathing-control system over- or under-reacts to a disturbance. A value above 1 makes breathing unstable and prone to central-apnoea cycles. Schwarz 2024, p.313

Apnoeic threshold

The CO₂ level below which the brain switches off the drive to breathe entirely — normally only 3–5 mmHg below a resting/eupnoeic CO₂ level. Central to how central apnoeas start and stop. Monograph, p.72–73

Cheyne-Stokes respiration (CSR)

A specific "crescendo-decrescendo" (rise-then-fall) breathing pattern with central apnoeas in between, classically seen in heart failure. Monograph, p.348

TECSA — Treatment-Emergent Central Sleep Apnoea

Also called "complex sleep apnoea": central apnoeas that newly appear once CPAP has opened up the airway and resolved the obstructive events. Usually settles within weeks in most patients. Schwarz 2024, p.311, 316

Arousal threshold

How easily a sleeping person wakes up in response to a breathing disturbance. A low threshold means waking too easily, before the throat muscles can properly re-engage. Monograph, p.72
